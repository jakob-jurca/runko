## Safety rules for the AI
- General sports nutrition only. No medical/clinical nutrition, no supplement dosing beyond caffeine/carbohydrate products, no weight-loss targets or calorie deficits.
- Never suggest restricting food to run faster. Signs of low energy availability (missed/irregular periods, repeated stress fractures, constant fatigue, rapid weight loss, preoccupation with food/weight) -> kindly recommend a doctor or sports dietitian. [Mountjoy 2023]
- If the runner mentions disordered eating: give no numbers, targets or plans anywhere in the conversation; respond with care and suggest professional support.
- Medical conditions (diabetes, kidney disease, pregnancy, GI disease): refer to their doctor/dietitian.
- Iron, vitamin D: suggest testing via a doctor if fatigued, female or high-volume; never prescribe doses.

## Daily carbohydrate needs (per kg body weight per day) [Thomas 2016; Burke 2011]
| Training load | Carbohydrate g/kg/day |
|---|---|
| Light (low intensity, < 1 h/day, or rest day) | 3-5 |
| Moderate (~1 h/day) | 5-7 |
| High (1-3 h/day moderate-high intensity) | 6-10 |
| Very high (> 4-5 h/day) | 8-12 |
Example 70 kg runner, 1 h/day: 350-490 g carbs/day.

## Protein and fat [Thomas 2016; Jäger 2017]
- Protein: 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day, spread over 4-5 meals of ~0.3 g/kg (20-40 g).
- Fat: ~20-35% of energy; not below 20%.

## Before a run [Thomas 2016; Kerksick 2017]
- Main meal 3-4 h before: 1-4 g/kg carbohydrate, moderate protein, low fat/fibre (70 kg: 70-280 g carbs).
- Snack 1-2 h before: ~1 g/kg carbs, easy to digest (banana, toast with jam, bread roll with honey).
- 30-60 min before: small carb snack or sports drink only if tolerated (15-30 g).
- Easy run < 60 min: fasted running is fine for most healthy runners; water available.
- Quality session or long run: eat carbs beforehand; fasted hard sessions reduce quality.
- Avoid high-fibre, high-fat, very spicy food and large amounts of dairy before running if GI issues occur.

## During a run: carbohydrate by duration [Thomas 2016; Jeukendrup 2014]
| Run duration | Carbohydrate |
|---|---|
| < 45 min | none needed |
| 45-75 min | small amounts or mouth rinse; optional |
| 1-2.5 h | 30-60 g/h |
| > 2.5-3 h | up to 60-90 g/h, using glucose + fructose (multiple transportable carbs, e.g. 2:1 or 1:0.8 ratio) |
- Start fuelling after ~30-45 min, then every 15-30 min in small doses. Gel ≈ 20-30 g carbs; 500 ml sports drink (6%) ≈ 30 g; banana ≈ 25 g.
- Train the gut: practise race fuelling in long runs for 4-8+ weeks; tolerance improves with practice (fewer GI symptoms, better absorption). [Jeukendrup 2017]
- Take gels with water, not with concentrated sports drink (GI distress).

## Hydration [Sawka 2007; Hew-Butler 2015]
- Before: 5-7 ml/kg ≥ 4 h before (70 kg ≈ 350-500 ml). If urine still dark or none: extra 3-5 ml/kg ~2 h before.
- During: drink to limit body mass loss to < 2%; do NOT gain weight. Typical 400-800 ml/h; higher for fast/heavy runners in heat, lower for slow/light runners in cool weather. Drinking to thirst is appropriate for most runs < 3 h and protects against overdrinking.
- Estimate sweat rate: weigh before and after a 60 min run (minimal clothing, towel dry); sweat loss (L) = kg lost + fluid drunk (L). Use to plan race drinking.
- Sodium: for runs > 2 h, salty sweaters or heat: ~300-600 mg sodium per hour (sports drinks, electrolyte tabs, salty snacks); individual sweat sodium varies widely.
- After: 1.25-1.5 L fluid per kg body mass lost, with sodium (food or drink). [Sawka 2007]
- Hyponatremia risk: slow runners (> 4 h marathon), drinking at every station, weight gain during race. Symptoms: nausea, headache, confusion, swelling -> medical help. Never "drink as much as possible". [Hew-Butler 2015]
- Urine colour: pale yellow = adequate.

## After a run (recovery) [Thomas 2016; Kerksick 2017]
- After easy runs < 60 min: normal next meal is enough.
- After hard sessions, long runs, or when training again within < 8-24 h: 1.0-1.2 g/kg carbs per hour for the first 4 h, starting within ~30-60 min; plus 0.25-0.3 g/kg protein (20-40 g) within ~2 h.
- Carbs + protein combo (e.g. 0.8 g/kg carbs + 0.4 g/kg protein) is sufficient when the carb target cannot be met. Example: 500 ml chocolate milk ≈ 50 g carbs + 17 g protein.
- The "recovery window" matters most when sessions are close together; with > 24 h between sessions, total daily intake matters more than timing.
- Pre-sleep protein (30-40 g casein, e.g. skyr, quark, cottage cheese) may support overnight muscle protein synthesis. [Jäger 2017]

## Race-day fuelling
- Carbohydrate loading (events > 90 min: marathon, half for slower runners): 10-12 g/kg/day for 36-48 h before, combined with the taper. Low-fibre carbs (white bread, rice, pasta, potatoes, juices, sweets). Expect +1-2 kg body weight (water stored with glycogen); normal. [Thomas 2016; Burke 2011]
- 5K-10K: normal diet; carb loading not needed (~7 g/kg the day before is enough).
- Race breakfast: 2-4 h before start; 1-4 g/kg carbs (70 kg at 2-3 g/kg = 140-210 g): white bread/toast with honey/jam, banana, rice, oats if tolerated; familiar foods only; 300-500 ml fluid.
- Marathon during race: 30-60 g/h for most, 60-90 g/h if gut-trained. Start ~30-45 min in. Plan gels around aid stations.
- Half marathon: 30-60 g/h optional; useful for runners slower than ~1:30.
- Nothing new on race day: food, gel brand, drink, caffeine; all tested in training.

## Caffeine [Guest 2021]
- Effective dose: 3-6 mg/kg body mass, ~60 min before; lower doses (~2-3 mg/kg) also work with fewer side effects. 70 kg: ~200 mg (≈ 2 espressos, or caffeinated gels).
- > 9 mg/kg: no extra benefit, more side effects (jitters, GI, poor sleep).
- Test in training; avoid for evening sessions if it disrupts sleep.

## Everyday eating principles
- Match energy intake to training load; eat more on hard/long days (periodise carbohydrates by day).
- Base diet: whole grains, fruit, vegetables, lean proteins, dairy/alternatives, healthy fats.
- Low energy availability (< ~30 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day) impairs hormones, bone and performance (REDs). [Mountjoy 2023]
- Iron: female runners, vegetarians and high-volume runners at higher risk of deficiency; fatigue and falling performance warrant a blood test by a doctor.
- Vitamin D: low status common in winter at Slovenian latitude (~46° N); testing via doctor if relevant.
- Alcohol: impairs rehydration, glycogen restoration and sleep; avoid in the recovery period after races and key sessions.
