## Safety rules for the AI (always apply)
- The AI is not a doctor. It never diagnoses. Phrase as "this sounds like it could be X; a physio or doctor can confirm".
- Default when unsure: reduce or stop running, recommend professional assessment.
- Any red flag below -> tell the runner to stop running and see a doctor; do not offer a training workaround first.
- Emergency signs (chest pain or pressure, fainting, severe breathlessness out of proportion, confusion during heat, sudden severe calf swelling or one-sided leg swelling with warmth): stop immediately, call emergency services (112 in Slovenia/EU).
- Background: 19-79% of runners report an injury per year; most are overuse from load increasing faster than tissue tolerance. [van Gent 2007; Nielsen 2014]

## Pain-monitoring rule (for mild, non-red-flag pain)
Based on the pain-monitoring model. [Silbernagel 2007]
- Pain 0-2/10 during running: acceptable; continue, monitor.
- Pain 3-5/10: modify (shorter, slower, flatter, softer surface, fewer days); acceptable ONLY if pain settles within 24 h and is not worse the next morning.
- Pain ≥ 6/10, or pain that increases during the run, or makes the runner limp or change stride: stop the run. No running until pain-free walking and daily activities; seek assessment if not improving in 1-2 weeks.
- Pain worse next morning than usual: next session reduced or replaced with cross-training.
- Rule: never run through pain that alters gait.

## Universal red flags (stop running, see a doctor)
- Sharp, localised pain on a bone (pain on pressing one spot), especially shin, foot, hip/groin.
- Pain at rest or at night.
- Pain that worsens as the run continues or persists while walking.
- Visible swelling, bruising, deformity, or a "pop" at onset.
- Numbness, tingling, weakness, or foot drop.
- Joint locking, giving way, or inability to bear weight.
- Fever, redness, warmth around a joint.
- Symptoms > 2-3 weeks despite reduced load.
- Repeat bone stress injury, or missed/irregular periods in female runners, or rapid weight loss (possible low energy availability, REDs). [Mountjoy 2023]

## Runner's knee (patellofemoral pain)
- Symptoms: dull ache around or behind the kneecap; worse on stairs (esp. down), squatting, downhills, after prolonged sitting ("theatre sign"). Usually gradual. [Crossley 2016]
- Likely causes: sudden volume increase; hills/downhills; weak hip and quadriceps muscles; overstriding.
- Continue running? Usually yes, modified, if pain ≤ 3-5/10 and settling within 24 h.
- Self-management: reduce weekly volume ~20-50% and avoid downhills; increase cadence 5-10% (reduces knee load) [Willy 2019]; hip + knee strengthening 2-3x/week (clamshells, side-lying hip abduction, step-downs, wall sits, leg press to pain-tolerable range) - strongest evidence-based treatment [Crossley 2016; Willy 2019]; short-term taping or foot orthoses can help.
- Typical course: weeks to a few months with exercise therapy.
- Red flags: swelling in the knee, locking, giving way, pain after a twist or fall, pain > 6 weeks without improvement -> physiotherapist/doctor.

## IT band syndrome (lateral knee pain)
- Symptoms: sharp or burning pain on the OUTSIDE of the knee; typically starts at a predictable time/distance into the run and worsens if continued; worse downhill; can be pain-free at rest. [Baker & Fredericson 2016]
- Likely causes: sudden volume increase, downhill running, running on cambered roads, weak hip abductors. Mechanism is compression of fat tissue under the band, not "tight band" friction. [Fairclough 2006]
- Continue running? Only to the point before pain starts; stop runs when lateral pain begins (running through tends to prolong it).
- Self-management: reduce volume to below pain-onset distance; avoid downhills and cambered surfaces; flat routes, run on alternate sides of road; increase cadence; hip abductor strengthening; cycling usually tolerated (check). Foam rolling may reduce symptoms short-term but does not "loosen" the ITB.
- Red flags: swelling, joint-line pain, locking/giving way, pain > 4-6 weeks despite modification -> assessment.

## Shin splints (medial tibial stress syndrome, MTSS)
- Symptoms: diffuse pain along the INNER lower edge of the shin over ≥ 5 cm; sore to press over a broad area; pain early in run that may ease during warm-up; common in beginners. [Winters 2013]
- Likely causes: rapid increase in volume, frequency or intensity; hard surfaces; new runners; returning runners; high BMI; female sex; low vitamin D/calcium status; poor bone loading history.
- Continue running? Reduce 30-50% if pain ≤ 3/10; rest from running if higher. Cross-train (cycling, pool).
- Self-management: reduce load and gradually rebuild; softer surfaces; calf strengthening (calf raises); check shoe condition; gradual return with walk-run.
- KEY distinction from stress fracture: MTSS = diffuse, ≥ 5 cm area. Stress fracture = focal pain on one spot (< 5 cm), pain when hopping on one leg, night pain -> red flag.
- Red flags: focal point tenderness, pain on hopping, night/rest pain, pain worsening despite rest; also exercise-induced tightness, numbness, or foot weakness (possible compartment syndrome) -> doctor.

## Plantar fasciitis (plantar heel pain)
- Symptoms: pain under the heel/inner heel; worst on first steps in the morning or after sitting; eases with walking, may return later in day. [Koc 2023]
- Likely causes: load increase, prolonged standing, calf tightness/limited ankle dorsiflexion, higher body mass, sudden change to minimalist shoes.
- Continue running? Often yes at reduced volume if pain ≤ 3-5/10 and settles; avoid hills and speedwork.
- Self-management: calf and plantar fascia stretching; high-load strength (single-leg heel raises with towel under toes, slow 3 s up/2 s top/3 s down, 3 sets of 8-12 every other day) [Rathleff 2015]; supportive/cushioned shoes; heel cups or taping short-term; avoid barefoot on hard floors. Recovery often takes 3-12 months; ~80-90% resolve with conservative care. [Koc 2023]
- Red flags: heel pain after a fall or jump, swelling, numbness/tingling in the foot, pain at night, pain in both heels with other joint pain -> doctor. No improvement after 6-8 weeks of self-care -> physio.

## Achilles tendinopathy
- Symptoms: pain and stiffness in the Achilles tendon (2-6 cm above heel = midportion; at the heel bone = insertional); stiff in the morning and at run start, may ease when warm, worse next day; tendon can be thickened and tender to squeeze. [Martin 2018]
- Likely causes: rapid increase in volume, hills or speedwork; switch to lower-drop shoes; weak calf; age > 35.
- Continue running? Yes, within the pain-monitoring rule (≤ 5/10 during, settled within 24 h, morning stiffness not worse) - loading is the treatment. [Silbernagel 2007]
- Self-management: remove hills, speedwork and jumping temporarily; calf loading programme daily or every other day (bent- and straight-knee heel raises, 3 x 15, progressing to weighted; eccentric heel drops Alfredson protocol 3 x 15 twice daily for 12 weeks [Alfredson 1998]); for insertional type avoid dropping heel below step level; do not stretch aggressively; heel lift short-term. Recovery: 3-6 months typical.
- Red flags: sudden sharp pain or "kick in the back of the leg" sensation, audible pop, inability to rise onto toes (possible rupture -> urgent same-day medical care); marked swelling or warmth; pain after fluoroquinolone antibiotic use.

## Stress fractures (bone stress injuries)
- Symptoms: gradual, then localised bone pain that worsens with running and eventually with walking; tender to press on one spot; pain on hopping; may cause night pain; sometimes swelling. [Warden 2014]
- Common sites: tibia (shin), metatarsals (foot), fibula, calcaneus. HIGH-RISK sites (poor healing): femoral neck (groin/hip pain), navicular (top of midfoot), anterior tibia, base of 5th metatarsal, sacrum/pelvis.
- Likely causes: rapid load increase; low energy availability (REDs) [Mountjoy 2023]; menstrual dysfunction; low vitamin D/calcium; previous stress fracture; low bone density; hard surfaces.
- Continue running? NO. Suspected stress fracture = stop running and see a doctor (imaging, usually MRI). Groin pain with running or hopping = urgent (femoral neck).
- Self-management while waiting for assessment: no running or jumping; non-impact cross-training only if pain-free (pool running, cycling); walking only if pain-free.
- Return to running only after medical clearance, typically 6-8 weeks (low-risk sites) to 3+ months (high-risk). Walk-run return plan, starting ~every other day.
- Doctor also checks energy availability, menstrual function, vitamin D, bone health when stress fractures recur.

## Return-to-run protocol (after injury, once pain-free walking for ~1 week and cleared if needed)
1. Pain-free tests first: brisk walk 30 min, 20 single-leg hops, 20 single-leg calf raises on injured side, all pain ≤ 2/10.
2. Start with walk-run every other day, e.g. 5 x (1 min run / 1 min walk), increasing run segments by 1 min per session if pain stays ≤ 2/10 during and next morning.
3. Reach 30 min continuous easy before adding a 4th weekly run, then before any quality session.
4. Rebuild weekly volume at ≤ 10%/week; no speed or hills for first 2-3 weeks of continuous running.
5. If pain returns > 3/10 or next morning is worse: step back to the previous stage.

## Prevention (general, for plan generation)
- Load progression rules in methodology.md (single run ≤ 10% longer than longest in 30 days).
- Strength training 2x/week: calves, quads, hips, hamstrings.
- Adequate energy and carbohydrate intake (see nutrition.md). Sleep 7-9 h.
- Change only one thing at a time: shoes, surface, volume, or intensity.
- Replace shoes when cushioning is visibly compressed or new aches appear (commonly ~500-800 km; evidence weak).
